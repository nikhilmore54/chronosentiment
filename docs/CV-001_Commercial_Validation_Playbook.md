## Purpose

This document makes Phase 1B executable. CS-R-015 defines *what* to validate. This document defines *how* to validate it — who to talk to, what to ask, how to move from interview to design partner, what constitutes success, and when to stop.

Phase 1B is a 90-day structured customer validation programme. Its output is a single decision: **go to MVP, reposition, or terminate.**

---

## Phase 1BA — Commercial Intelligence Workstream

Phase 1BA is a zero-contact intelligence phase that precedes direct customer outreach. It does not replace primary validation — it makes every subsequent interview more targeted and increases the information gained from each conversation.

```
Phase 1A — Secondary Research (complete)
        ↓
Phase 1BA — Commercial Intelligence (zero-contact)
        ↓
Phase 1B — Primary Validation (interviews, demos, PoC)
        ↓
MVP go/no-go decision
```

The goal of Phase 1BA is to arrive at every customer conversation knowing almost as much about the firm as the interviewee expects you to. This is especially important when operating as a solo founder with limited access to warm introductions — informed questions signal credibility and generate better evidence.

### The Staged Evidence Funnel

Rather than treating Phase 1B as "30 interviews," treat it as an evidence acquisition programme. The funnel below shows how to earn access to harder-to-reach evidence sources:

```
Commercial Intelligence (zero-contact, Phase 1BA)
        ↓
Public Evidence (OBS — conference panels, webinars, podcasts)
        ↓
Expert Interviews (EXP — consultants, former CIOs, compliance advisers)
        ↓
Warm Introductions (from experts and referrals)
        ↓
Target Customer Interviews (INT — CIOs, PMs, compliance officers)
        ↓
Product Demonstrations (DEM — structured feedback sessions)
        ↓
Prototype Evaluations (POC — hands-on use with real decisions)
        ↓
Design Partners
```

The later stages are harder to access. You earn your way there by demonstrating knowledge and credibility at each earlier stage.

### Phase 1BA Intelligence Dossier

For each target firm, collect the following before making contact. All fields are based on public information; clearly distinguish inference from confirmed fact.

| Field | Sources |
|-------|---------|
| Firm name, AUM, investment strategy | Firm website, FCA register, Companies House, Bloomberg |
| CIO / Head of Investment name and background | LinkedIn, firm website, conference speaker lists |
| Investment team size | LinkedIn, firm website |
| AI adoption signals | Job postings mentioning AI tools, conference presentations, press mentions |
| Technology stack (where public) | Job postings, LinkedIn profiles of technology staff |
| Existing vendors | Job postings, LinkedIn, press releases |
| Regulatory exposure | FCA register, annual reports, regulatory filings |
| Recent news and commentary | Google News, LinkedIn activity, podcast appearances |
| Hiring activity | LinkedIn Jobs, Indeed — signals of growth or capability gaps |
| Public commentary on AI or investment processes | Conference recordings, podcasts, webinars |
| Estimated urgency | Inference from regulatory exposure + AI adoption signals |
| Estimated WTP | Inference from AUM, team size, existing vendor spend |
| Suggested outreach angle | Based on the above — what specific problem to reference |

**Target:** Complete dossiers for 100–200 firms before beginning outreach. Prioritise Tier 1 firms (Independent Asset Managers £500M–£5B).

### Evidence Sources for Phase 1BA

**Public observation sources (OBS records):**
- CFA Institute conferences and webinars
- Institutional Investor events
- FundForum International
- SuperReturn
- Investment Management Association (IMA) events
- Podcasts: Capital Allocators, Invest Like the Best, The Acquirers Podcast, Flirting with Models
- LinkedIn Live sessions by CIOs and investment leaders
- Regulatory consultation responses (FCA, SEC, ESMA)

**Expert interview targets (EXP records):**
- Consultants serving asset managers (e.g. Mercer, Willis Towers Watson, bfinance)
- Technology implementation partners (firms that implement Bloomberg, FactSet, or AlphaSense)
- Compliance advisers specialising in investment management
- Former CIOs or Heads of Investment (now independent or at advisory firms)
- Recruiters specialising in investment management (CIO, PM, compliance roles)
- Industry analysts covering investment management technology (Celent, Aite-Novarica, Datos Insights)

One experienced consultant may have visibility across 50 firms. Their evidence is not the same as customer validation, but it can rapidly improve your understanding of the market and identify better interview targets.

### Access Strategy for Solo Founders

Cold-emailing CIOs of £500M–£5B asset managers from India is likely to produce a low response rate. The following strategies increase access without requiring warm introductions:

**1. Lead with the product, not the research.**
> "We've built a prototype that reconstructs investment decisions and generates explainable decision records. I'd value 30 minutes of your feedback on whether this addresses real operational problems."

People respond better to reacting to something concrete than to abstract market research.

**2. Use conference Q&A and LinkedIn as observation sources first.**
Before reaching out to a specific CIO, find their public statements on AI, governance, or investment process. Reference those statements in your outreach — it demonstrates you've done your homework.

**3. Build through experts before customers.**
Reach experts (consultants, former CIOs, compliance advisers) first. They are more accessible, have cross-firm visibility, and can provide warm introductions to target customers.

**4. Use referrals aggressively.**
Every interview should end with: "Is there anyone else you'd recommend I speak to?" One referral from a credible source is worth ten cold emails.

**5. Redefine the Phase 1B success metric.**
Given access constraints, avoid making "30 customer interviews" the primary success measure. Instead, focus on evidence quality:
- 8–12 in-depth customer interviews (INT)
- 10–15 expert interviews (EXP)
- Commercial intelligence database covering 100–200 target firms
- 2–3 product demonstrations (DEM)
- 1 prototype evaluation (POC)
- 1 design partner

That evidence base is sufficient to make a well-grounded go/no-go decision, and is achievable from India without a pre-existing network.

## 1. Target Customer Ranking

Not all potential customers are equally valuable for Phase 1B. The goal is to find firms where the problem is most acute, the buying process is fastest, and the reference value is highest.

### Tier 1 — Primary Targets (aim for 15+ of 20–30 interviews)

**Independent Asset Managers, AUM £500M–£5B, 5–25 investment professionals**

Why:
- Large enough to have structured investment processes; small enough that those processes are not yet fully systematised
- Fast internal decision-making — no legacy IT procurement process
- CIO is often also a portfolio manager — single buyer with both the problem and the budget authority
- Regulatory pressure is real (EU AI Act, FCA guidance) but not yet absorbed by a large compliance team
- Reference value is high — peers trust peers in this segment

Target geography: UK, Europe, US (in that order for initial outreach — EU AI Act creates more immediate urgency in UK/Europe)

Ideal profile: Firm using AI tools (ChatGPT, Claude, or purpose-built financial AI) in investment workflow; has experienced knowledge loss from staff turnover; CIO is personally involved in investment committee

---

### Tier 2 — Secondary Targets (aim for 5–10 interviews)

**Family Offices, AUM £200M–£2B**

Why:
- Governance pressure from family principals and external advisors
- Often have no compliance infrastructure — the CIO is also the compliance function
- High willingness to pay if the value proposition is clear
- Longer sales cycles than Tier 1 but higher ACV potential

Caution: Family offices are heterogeneous — some are highly sophisticated, others are not. Screen for AI tool usage before investing interview time.

---

### Tier 3 — Validation Targets (aim for 3–5 interviews)

**Hedge Funds, AUM £500M–£5B**

Why:
- High AI adoption; high governance pressure from prime brokers and investors
- Faster decision-making than traditional asset managers
- Potentially higher willingness to pay

Caution: Hedge funds have strong "not invented here" tendencies and may prefer to build internal tools. Use these interviews primarily to test whether the problem resonates in a different context, not as primary sales targets.

---

### Tier 4 — Horizon Targets (do not prioritise in Phase 1B)

**Large Institutional Asset Managers, AUM > £10B**

Why not now:
- Long procurement cycles (12–24 months)
- Large compliance and technology teams who will want to evaluate extensively
- May have existing internal solutions or vendor relationships
- Not the right beachhead — too slow for Phase 1B validation

Include 1–2 interviews for market intelligence, not as sales targets.

## 2. The Buying Committee

Understanding who buys, who approves, who blocks, and who influences is essential for Phase 1B. The goal is to map this at 10+ firms.

### Typical buying committee at a Tier 1 firm

```
Portfolio Manager / Senior Analyst
(Identifies the problem; primary user; internal champion)
        ↓
CIO / Head of Investment
(Primary buyer; approves budget; most important relationship)
        ↓
Compliance Officer
(Validates regulatory fit; can block if not satisfied; secondary buyer)
        ↓
Head of Technology / IT
(Evaluates integration; can slow the process; not a buyer)
        ↓
CFO / Finance
(Approves contract; involved late in the process)
```

### Who to target first

**Start with the CIO.** At Tier 1 firms, the CIO is typically the primary buyer and has the problem most acutely. They chair the investment committee, are responsible for LP reporting, and have experienced knowledge loss directly.

**Do not start with compliance.** Compliance officers are important validators but rarely initiate software purchases. Starting with compliance frames ChronoSentiment as a compliance tool — the wrong framing.

**Do not start with technology.** Technology teams evaluate; they do not buy. Starting with technology creates a long evaluation process without a commercial champion.

### Signals that you have the right buyer

- They describe the problem unprompted before you explain the product
- They have a specific recent example of the problem (a decision they couldn't explain, a staff departure that took context with it)
- They have budget authority or direct access to the person who does
- They are willing to introduce you to their compliance officer

## 3. Discovery Interview Guide

### Before the interview

**Research the firm:** AUM, team size, investment strategy, any public statements about AI use or governance. Do not ask questions you could have answered with 10 minutes of research.

**Set the right frame:** This is a research conversation, not a sales call. You are trying to understand their world, not pitch a product. Say so explicitly at the start.

**Target duration:** 45–60 minutes. Do not run over without permission.

---

### Opening (5 minutes)

> "Thank you for making time. I want to be upfront: we're in the early stages of building a product for investment teams, and we're spending the next 90 days talking to CIOs and portfolio managers before we write a line of code. I'm not here to pitch anything — I'm here to understand how your team makes investment decisions and what, if anything, is broken about that process. Is that a useful way to spend the next 45 minutes?"

This framing:
- Establishes credibility (you're doing research, not selling)
- Sets expectations (no pitch)
- Gives them permission to be honest about problems

---

### Problem discovery (20 minutes)

Ask these questions in order. Do not skip ahead. Do not explain the product until Section 3 of this guide.

**1. Walk me through how a typical investment decision gets made at your firm — from initial idea to execution.**

*Listen for:* Where does the process break down? Where is information lost? Where is the reasoning informal?

**2. When you look back at a decision six months later — a position you've closed, or one that went wrong — how do you reconstruct what you were thinking at the time?**

*Listen for:* Do they have a system? Do they rely on memory? Do they describe this as a problem?

**3. Have you ever had a situation where you couldn't explain a past decision to an LP, a regulator, or a new team member? What happened?**

*Listen for:* Specific examples. Emotional response. Whether this is a live problem or a theoretical one.

**4. How are you using AI tools in your investment process today?**

*Listen for:* Which tools, which workflows, how integrated. Do not lead with governance — let them describe their AI usage first.

**5. When an AI tool contributes to an investment recommendation — a summary, a signal, a suggested trade — how do you track that contribution?**

*Listen for:* Do they have any system? Do they recognise this as a gap? Do they describe it as a problem?

**6. If a regulator asked you to demonstrate that your AI usage in investment decisions is documented and auditable, what would you show them?**

*Listen for:* Confidence or anxiety. Whether they have a current answer or are improvising.

---

### Problem validation (10 minutes)

If the previous questions have surfaced genuine problems, probe deeper. If not, do not force it — note that the problem did not resonate and move on.

**7. On a scale of 1–10, how much of a problem is [the specific problem they described]? What would make it a 10?**

**8. What have you tried to solve it? What worked? What didn't?**

*Listen for:* Have they tried to solve it? With what? Why did it fail? This tells you whether the problem is painful enough to drive action.

**9. If this problem were solved tomorrow, what would be different about how your team operates?**

*Listen for:* Concrete operational changes vs vague aspirations. Concrete changes indicate genuine urgency.

---

### Solution concept (10 minutes)

Only introduce the product concept if the problem has been validated. If the problem did not resonate, skip this section and close the interview.

> "Based on what you've described, I want to share a concept we're working on and get your reaction. It's not a finished product — it's a direction we're exploring."

Describe the Decision Workspace concept in 2–3 sentences:

> "We're building a system where every investment decision has a structured record — the thesis, the evidence available at the time, the AI tools that contributed, the approval, and the outcome. The goal is that six months later, you can open any decision and understand exactly why it was made, with a reconstruction of the information environment at the time. You can generate an LP-ready explanation in minutes rather than days."

Then ask:

**10. What's your reaction to that?**

*Listen for:* Immediate recognition ("that's exactly what we need") vs polite interest vs confusion. Do not interpret polite interest as validation.

**11. What would you call this internally? If you were describing this to your compliance officer or your board, what language would you use?**

*This is the category language test. Do not suggest language — let them generate it.*

**12. Who else in your firm would care about this?**

*Listen for:* Does it map to the buying committee you expect? Are there stakeholders you hadn't anticipated?

---

### Commercial probe (5 minutes)

Only ask these questions if the solution concept resonated. Do not ask them if the problem did not resonate — it will feel premature and damage the relationship.

**13. If this existed today and worked as described, is this something your firm would consider buying?**

*Listen for:* "Yes, definitely" vs "maybe" vs "probably not." Do not accept "maybe" as validation.

**14. What would you need to see before you'd be willing to pay for it?**

*Listen for:* Proof-of-concept requirements, reference customers, integration requirements, pricing expectations.

**15. What would you expect to pay for something like this annually?**

*Do not anchor with a number. Let them name a figure first. If they won't, offer a range: "We're thinking somewhere between £20,000 and £100,000 per year — does that feel right, too high, or too low?"*

---

### Close (5 minutes)

**16. Is there anyone else you'd recommend I speak to — either at your firm or at other firms you respect?**

*Referrals are the most efficient source of qualified interviews.*

**17. Would you be open to seeing a proof-of-concept when we have one — something you could react to with real decisions from your firm?**

*This is the design partner probe. A "yes" here is a strong signal.*

---

### What not to ask

- Do not ask "would you use this?" — people say yes to avoid conflict
- Do not ask "is this a problem?" — people say yes to be polite
- Do not ask leading questions that contain the answer ("don't you find it hard to explain decisions to LPs?")
- Do not ask about features — ask about problems
- Do not ask about competitors by name — it frames the conversation as a sales call

---

### Recognising false positives

The most dangerous outcome of Phase 1B is false validation — interviews that feel positive but do not represent genuine purchase intent.

**Signs of a false positive:**
- They describe the problem in your language rather than their own
- They say "yes" to everything without specific examples
- They are enthusiastic about the concept but vague about who would buy it
- They say "we'd definitely use this" but won't commit to a design partnership
- They describe the problem as important but not urgent ("we should probably do something about this")

**Signs of genuine validation:**
- They describe the problem with a specific recent example before you explain the product
- They use their own language to describe the problem (not yours)
- They name a specific person who would approve the budget
- They ask about pricing unprompted
- They ask when they can see a demo or proof-of-concept
- They offer to introduce you to their compliance officer or board

## 4. Design Partner Process

A design partner is a firm that agrees to use ChronoSentiment for real investment decisions during the proof-of-concept phase, in exchange for early access, influence over the product direction, and a reference relationship.

The design partner relationship is the most important commercial outcome of Phase 1B. One genuine design partner is worth more than 30 positive interviews.

### The progression

```
Interview (45–60 min)
        ↓
Follow-up (share concept document; ask for referrals)
        ↓
Demo (30 min; show proof-of-concept when available)
        ↓
Design Partner Conversation (define the relationship)
        ↓
Proof-of-Concept (use the product for real decisions; structured feedback)
        ↓
Pilot (paid; 3–6 months; structured success criteria)
        ↓
Reference Customer
```

### Design partner conversation

When a firm has expressed strong interest after the demo, have an explicit conversation about the design partner relationship:

> "We're looking for 2–3 firms to work with closely during the proof-of-concept phase. In exchange for early access, we'd ask for structured feedback sessions every two weeks, permission to use your experience (anonymised) as a case study, and a reference conversation with future customers if the product works for you. Does that sound like something your firm would be interested in?"

### Design partner criteria

A firm qualifies as a design partner if:
- They have confirmed the problem is real and active at their firm
- They have a named individual who will use the product for real decisions
- They have a named individual who has budget authority for a future paid contract
- They are willing to commit to structured feedback sessions (2x per month)
- They are willing to provide a reference if the product works

### Design partner red flags

- They want to "evaluate" without committing to structured feedback
- They want to use the product but won't commit to a reference
- The named user is a junior analyst, not a portfolio manager or CIO
- They have no budget authority and no path to the person who does

## 5. Pricing Experiment

The goal of the pricing experiment is to establish the willingness-to-pay range for the target segment before setting a price. Do not set a price before running this experiment.

### The Van Westendorp method (recommended)

Ask four questions in sequence, after the solution concept has been validated:

1. "At what price would you consider this product to be so expensive that you would not consider buying it, regardless of quality?" *(Too expensive)*

2. "At what price would you consider this product to be so inexpensive that you would question its quality?" *(Too cheap)*

3. "At what price would you consider this product to be starting to get expensive, but you'd still consider it?" *(Expensive but acceptable)*

4. "At what price would you consider this product to be a bargain — a great buy for the money?" *(Cheap but acceptable)*

Run this with at least 10 qualified prospects (firms where the problem has been validated). Plot the four curves to find the acceptable price range and the optimal price point.

### Pricing anchors from comparable platforms

| Platform | Category | Indicative ACV |
|----------|----------|----------------|
| AlphaSense | Research intelligence | US$15,000–US$50,000/seat/yr |
| FactSet | Data and analytics | US$12,000–US$30,000/seat/yr |
| Fiddler AI | ML governance | US$50,000–US$200,000/firm/yr |
| Arthur AI | AI governance | US$60,000–US$250,000/firm/yr |

**Working hypothesis:** US$30,000–US$80,000/firm/yr for Tier 1 targets. This is Confidence D — requires Phase 1B validation.

### Pricing objection responses

| Objection | Response |
|-----------|---------|
| "That's expensive for an early-stage product" | "We're pricing based on the value of the problem solved, not the age of the company. What would it cost you to reconstruct a decision record manually for an LP audit?" |
| "We'd need to see it work first" | "That's exactly why we're offering a proof-of-concept. The pricing conversation is for after you've seen it work." |
| "We could build this internally" | "What would that cost in engineering time? And would it be ready before your next regulatory review?" |
| "We don't have budget for this" | "Where does this problem currently sit in your budget? Is it a compliance cost, a technology cost, or an investment operations cost?" |

## 6. Kill Criteria

Phase 1B must produce a clear go/no-go decision. The following criteria define when to stop, reposition, or proceed.

### Stop (terminate the investment thesis)

Terminate if, after 30 interviews:

- Fewer than 3 firms confirm the problem is real, active, and not currently solved
- No firm is willing to engage as a design partner
- Willingness to pay is consistently below US$15,000/yr
- Multiple firms describe the problem as already solved by existing tools or internal processes

**What termination means:** The commercial demand is insufficient to sustain a dedicated product at this time. The technical platform has value but the product framing is wrong. Options: pivot to a different customer segment, pivot to a different problem framing, or pause and revisit when market conditions change.

### Reposition (change the framing, not the product)

Reposition if:

- The problem resonates but the category language does not ("decision governance" does not resonate; customers use different language)
- The problem resonates with a different segment than expected (e.g., hedge funds more than asset managers)
- The primary buyer is different from expected (e.g., compliance officer rather than CIO)
- Willingness to pay is real but below the target range (US$15,000–US$30,000/yr rather than US$30,000–US$80,000/yr)

**What repositioning means:** Adjust the go-to-market strategy, category language, and target segment before proceeding to MVP. Do not change the product — change how it is described and to whom it is sold.

### Proceed to MVP (go decision)

Proceed if all four minimum criteria are met:

1. At least 5 of 20+ interviewed firms confirm the problem is real, active, and not currently solved by existing tools
2. At least 3 firms express willingness to pay at or above US$30,000/yr
3. At least 1 firm agrees to a design partnership
4. Proof-of-concept demonstrates core integration is feasible within a 6-month engineering timeline

**What proceeding means:** Begin MVP development with the design partner as the primary customer. The go-to-market strategy is validated enough to justify the engineering investment.

### Decision timeline

| Week | Milestone |
|------|-----------|
| 1–2 | Outreach and scheduling (target 30 firms contacted) |
| 3–6 | First 15 interviews; calibration review after interview 10 |
| 7–10 | Remaining 15 interviews; proof-of-concept build begins |
| 11–12 | Design partner conversations; pricing experiment |
| 13 | Phase 1B evidence review; go/no-go decision |

## 7. Evidence Recording

Every interview must be recorded in the Phase 1B Evidence Ledger (to be created as a separate document). The minimum record for each interview:

| Field | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Firm | Name, AUM, team size, investment strategy |
| Interviewee | Name, title, role in buying committee |
| Date | Interview date |
| Problem resonance | 1–5 scale; specific examples cited Y/N |
| AI tool usage | Which tools, which workflows |
| Solution reaction | Immediate recognition / polite interest / confusion |
| Category language | Exact words they used to describe the problem |
| WTP signal | Named a figure Y/N; range if named |
| Design partner interest | Strong / weak / none |
| Referrals | Names and firms referred |
| Key quotes | 1–3 verbatim quotes that capture their perspective |
| Hypothesis updates | Which of H1–H7 does this interview support or challenge? |

After every 5 interviews, review the evidence ledger and update the hypothesis confidence levels. Do not wait until the end of Phase 1B to synthesise.
